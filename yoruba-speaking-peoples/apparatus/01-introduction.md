# Introduction to the Critical Edition

Lieutenant-Colonel Alfred Burdon Ellis (1852–1894) was an officer of the First West India Regiment and one of the most prolific colonial ethnographic observers of late nineteenth-century West Africa. Published in London in 1894, *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast of West Africa* formed the third and most influential volume in Ellis’s major ethnographic trilogy, following his earlier studies of the Tshi-speaking (Akan) and Gã-speaking peoples.

## The Colonial Lens and Indigenous Reality

Ellis operated in an era marked by Victorian racial theories and colonial administrative expansion. He did not speak Yoruba natively, relying instead on bilingual mission-educated informants, government interpreters, and his own administrative observations along the Lagos colony and the Bight of Benin. Consequently, his work reflects both the keen observational rigor of nineteenth-century comparative anthropology and the inevitable biases and misapprehensions of the colonial gazetteer.

Yet, despite these colonial filters, Ellis’s survey holds immense historiographical value. Ellis meticulously documented:
- **The Orisha Pantheon**: Detailed descriptions of the functions, priesthoods, sacred symbols, and sacrificial rites associated with Olorun, Obatala, Odudua, Shango, Ogun, Oshun, and Ifa.
- **The System of Divination**: One of the earliest European attempts to document the sixteen principal Odus of Ifa divination.
- **Social Structure and Customary Law**: Hereditary kinship systems, blood-feuds, tribal markings (*Ila*), land tenure, marriage ceremonies, and funeral rites.
- **Proverbs and Folklore**: Over 250 traditional Yoruba proverbs and fables recorded in both the original vernacular and English translation.

## The Value of Pairing Ellis with Johnson

For modern scholars, students, and readers of Yoruba cultural history, Ellis provides an invaluable comparative mirror to the Reverend Samuel Johnson’s *The History of the Yorubas*. Where Johnson writes from within the sacred royal traditions of the Oyo interior, Ellis captures the coastal perspective, colonial interaction, and the external anthropological view of the same turbulent decade. Read together with critical discernment, the two works offer a panoramic understanding of nineteenth-century Yoruba thought and society.

## About This Restored Edition

This edition has been restored with an active critical apparatus designed for modern readers:
- **Preserved and Corrected Text**: Cleaned of scanning defects while faithfully preserving Ellis’s original structure, appendices, and comparative notes.
- **Critical Cultural Glossary**: A reference guide clarifying nineteenth-century anglicized spellings of Yoruba divinities and concepts.
- **Historical Context**: Contextual notes highlighting where modern scholarship corrects Victorian anthropological assumptions.
